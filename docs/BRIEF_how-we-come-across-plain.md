# How We Want to Come Across — Plain Version

- **Requested by:** operator, via Telegram, 2026-09-30 — feedback on
  `docs/BRIEF_silicon-jungle-the-why.md` and the operator's own draft, "How We Want
  to Come Across": both still read as too jargon-heavy and academic for clear
  communication. This is a rewrite aimed at fixing that specific complaint.
- **What changed, and why:** the earlier drafts had good lines in them, but they
  were wrapped in a framework — numbered "cups," a translation table, a scoring
  checklist, audience-segmented message matrices. Frameworks are themselves a kind
  of jargon, even when every individual word is simple: they make a reader feel like
  they're in a workshop, not a conversation. This version keeps the good lines and
  drops the framework. It's meant to be read once, straight through, like someone
  talking.

## We want to come across like a good host, not a salesperson

A good host knows the house, is glad you came, and isn't worried about whether you
stay. A salesperson talks about what they built. A host talks about what's going to
happen to you. That's the whole difference, and it's the only rule underneath
everything else here.

## The story, plainly

Todd spent thirty-eight years learning how to read an organization — a real method,
used on more than five thousand companies, on five continents. It ended up locked
inside old software almost nobody can open anymore, and Todd waited more than ten
years for someone who could bring it back to life.

We knew that kind of waiting. For almost two years, one of us had been building a
company alone, behind a screen, and had stopped moving.

So a group of builders got in a room for a weekend. By Sunday, Todd's life's work
could talk. You'd speak to it for fifteen minutes, and it would ask you real
follow-up questions and hand you back a reading of your organization that used to
take a hundred and eighty questions on paper — the kind almost nobody finishes.
Todd watched it happen. And the other stalled project came back to life in the same
room, the same weekend.

That's the whole reason any of this keeps happening. We don't do it to make one
more product. We do it to bring stuck work back to life.

## What we've actually done, no more and no less

Eight weekends have happened. Four people became more than twenty, all building
together. Two things grew up out of those weekends and turned around to help run
them: Toddito, which reads organizations, and Keonda, which is starting to
remember who in the community can help who.

That's it. That's not a metaphor for a bigger claim — it's the actual, current
size of the thing.

## What's still ahead, said plainly

Keonda isn't done — it doesn't yet fully remember who can help whom across the
whole community. There's no reliable path yet from "came to a weekend" to "built a
real company." We don't have a home that's full, with more people wanting in than
we have room for. And we don't yet have proof in the form that matters most —
paying customers, partnerships that hold.

We say this plainly because the honesty is the actual asset. Eight real weekends
and a proven leap for Toddito are true and worth saying with confidence. Saying
what's still missing, calmly, is what makes the rest believable.

## The one line, if there's only room for one

Some of the best work in the world is stuck, waiting for the right room. We build
the room.

## The slightly longer version, if someone wants it

We help people who already have their customers' trust turn it into a business,
starting with one weekend. Code got cheap — anyone can build something in a
weekend now. Trust didn't get any cheaper. So we spend our time near the people
who already have it, and give them a room, a weekend, and the tools to build.

## How to actually talk about it, five habits

1. **Talk about the other person, not about what we built.** They're the one
   doing something real. We're glad to be there for it.
2. **Say three things, then stop.** If someone wants more, they'll ask. Answering
   a question nobody asked yet is the fastest way to sound like a pitch.
3. **Say what isn't finished yet, in the same calm voice as what is.** It's more
   convincing, not less — a claim with no gaps in it reads as a sales pitch, and
   people can tell.
4. **Talk about where someone's going, not what they're escaping.** "Bring your
   name and your customers, leave with a company" works better than "stop
   struggling alone."
5. **End with something small.** Never "join the platform." Just: come to the
   next weekend. A small, easy yes.
